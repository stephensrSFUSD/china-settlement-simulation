How the simulation decides (for teachers)

Every grid square has hidden data. Elevation and roughness are read from the colors of the map. Rivers, rainfall, temperature, soil, and natural hazards come from a geographic data layer lined up with the map.

Each turn is 50 years (40 turns in all). A town's population grows toward what its land can feed. Crowded towns spread into nearby squares. Flat, fertile squares are cheap to settle; mountains, desert, cold forest, and tropical forest cost much more. Navigable rivers make spreading easier. Distance from the first town makes it harder.

Disasters depend on place: floods on the lower Yellow, Huai and Yangtze rivers; drought in the dry north; typhoons on the southeast coast; earthquakes along fault zones; disease in the hot wet south; raids near the northern grasslands; hard winters in the far north.

When two towns' lands touch, each side chooses to trade, fight, or guard the border. Both must choose trade for trade to begin, and each partner gains more when the other has goods it lacks. If either side fights, there is war, and the stronger side slowly takes land (rough land and guarded borders are harder to take). In the "review" setting, the choice comes back every 500 years, wars can end if both offer peace every 300 years, and long-time trading partners may unite. "Let the dice choose" plays a side automatically.

Army strength depends mostly on population, then on technology (which grows with time and trade). Herding peoples get a cavalry bonus. Everyone starts with stone and wood weapons. From 1700 BCE, a town with both copper and tin (on its land or through trade) gets a small bronze bonus. From 1000 BCE bronze is common, and any town with one of the metals or any trading partner has it. From 600 BCE, a town with iron ore on its land or a trading partner with iron gets a larger bonus (about 30% in war, 8% in food), which replaces the bronze bonus.

Each town also gets a hidden luck roll. The same luck number always gives the same dice, so students can move one town and compare fairly. The "In real history" notes compare each town's region and result with what happened there between about 2000 BCE and 1 CE.
